Assistive listening for rooms that do not have an AV department

A community hall, a parish church, a small council chamber, a village theatre, a pharmacy counter. Rooms with a modest budget, no in-house AV staff, and a real obligation to be usable by people with hearing loss. This site is written for whoever has been handed that problem.

Assistive listening is not a loudspeaker turned up. Amplifying a room more does not help someone with hearing loss, because the room's reverberation and background noise get amplified along with the speech. What helps is delivering the sound directly to the listener's ears, clean, with the room taken out of the path. Every assistive listening technology is a different way of doing that one thing.

Of those technologies, the audio frequency induction loop — the hearing loop — has one property none of the others has: it works with the hearing aid the listener is already wearing, with no receiver to collect, no battery to charge, and no queue at a desk. That is why it remains the default recommendation for public spaces, and why it is worth understanding properly before choosing something else.

The four questions

How does a hearing loop actually work?
Magnetic fields, telecoils, the "T" position, what the listener experiences, and an honest comparison with FM, infrared, and Wi-Fi or Bluetooth-based systems.
What decides whether a loop will work in this room?
Perimeter versus phased-array designs, metal loss in modern buildings, overspill into adjacent rooms, driver sizing, cable, and the source-audio problem that sinks more installations than any field-strength issue.
How do I know it works?
Why an installation is not finished until it is measured, what IEC 60118-4 specifies, what a field-strength meter does, and the walk-test that any venue can perform without one.
What if a loop is the wrong answer here?
When infrared or a Wi-Fi/Bluetooth system fits better, what Auracast changes, and the signage and staff training that determine whether any of it ever gets used.

The failure that matters most

Most non-working assistive listening systems are installed, switched on, and unused Survey after survey of venues finds systems that are broken, muted, unplugged, or working perfectly while nobody on staff knows they exist. The technical work is the easy half. The half that determines whether anyone benefits is: a sign people can see, a member of staff who knows how to switch it on, a microphone that is actually in the signal path, and a check that happens on a schedule rather than when someone complains.

A minimum viable programme for a small venue

  1. Establish what the room is for. One person speaking into a microphone is a very different problem from an open discussion where nobody uses one. If nothing goes through a microphone, no assistive listening system can help, and the first fix is microphones.
  2. Pick the technology on the room, not the price list. See signage and alternatives for the trade-offs.
  3. Have it commissioned and measured against the applicable standard, by someone with a calibrated meter, and keep the report. See verifying a loop.
  4. Sign it using the recognised symbol, at the entrance and at the point of use, and say plainly what to do — which switch on a hearing aid, or where to collect a receiver.
  5. Train the people at the door, and write the instructions somewhere they will be found in two years when those people have left.
  6. Test it on a schedule, log it, and fix faults as faults rather than as complaints.

Scope and limits

This site explains principles and terminology so that a venue can specify work, ask useful questions, and check what it has been given. It does not attempt to substitute for a competent installer, for the published text of the relevant standards, or for legal advice.

Accessibility obligations for venues are set by law and differ substantially by jurisdiction — and in many places they specify not just that a system exists, but its coverage, capacity, and signage. Find the rule that applies to your building and read it. Where this site cites a figure from a standard, treat it as an orientation point and confirm it against the current published version of that standard.
