Signage, staff, and choosing the right technology

A system nobody knows about serves nobody. This page covers the two things that decide real-world usage — knowing it exists and knowing how to use it — and the cases where a loop is not the right choice.

Signage

There is an internationally recognised symbol for assistive listening: a stylised ear with a diagonal bar through it, and, where an induction loop is provided, the letter T alongside it. It is widely understood by hearing aid users, who are the audience that matters. Use it rather than inventing your own graphic.

  • Sign the entrance and the point of use. Someone deciding whether to come in needs to know at the door; someone choosing a seat needs to know in the room.
  • Say what to do. "Hearing loop fitted — switch your hearing aid to the T setting" is more useful than a symbol alone. For receiver-based systems, say where to collect one and whether anything is required to borrow it.
  • Say where it works. If only part of the room is covered, or only the counter, say so. An overstated sign is worse than none, because it sends someone to a seat where nothing works and tells them the fault is theirs.
  • Put it on the website too, on the accessibility or visit-us page. People plan ahead, and a venue that publishes what it has is a venue people trust enough to try.
  • Take signs down when the system is broken, and put them back when it is fixed. Then fix it quickly.

Staff

The most common assistive listening failure is a working system that no one on shift can switch on or explain. The fix is small and specific:

  • A one-page instruction, laminated, physically with the equipment. Where the switch is, what the indicator should show, which microphone feeds it, and who to call.
  • Include it in induction for every front-of-house role, not just technical staff. The person who gets asked is the person at the door.
  • Teach one sentence: "We have a hearing loop — please switch your hearing aid to the T setting." That sentence is most of the training.
  • Teach the counter loop too. A counter loop only works if the staff member speaks into its microphone; standing back from it and talking loudly defeats the entire installation.
  • Name an owner for the monthly check and the annual professional verification, as a role rather than a person, so it survives staff turnover.
The rest of accessible listening Assistive listening sits alongside, not instead of, other measures: good acoustics and reduced reverberation, controlling background noise, lighting that lets people see the speaker's face for lipreading, sightlines to the talker, captioning or speech-to-text where it is available, and the simple discipline of insisting that speakers use the microphone even when they are sure they do not need it. That last one is free and is frequently the biggest single improvement in a small hall.

When a loop is not the right answer

SituationConsiderWhy
Confidentiality is essential — consulting rooms, interview rooms, courts Infrared Infrared light does not pass through walls, so containment is inherent rather than designed. A low-spill loop array can also work, but must be measured for containment specifically.
Severe metal loss and no viable loop route Infrared, radio, or a Wi-Fi/Bluetooth-based system Where the structure defeats the field, more current is not a strategy. Verify with a temporary test loop before concluding either way.
Coverage needed across many rooms or a whole site Radio, or a network-based system Looping every space is expensive; a distributed system with issued receivers may cost less overall, at the price of managing receivers.
Multiple simultaneous channels — languages, audio description Infrared or radio, multi-channel A single loop carries one programme. Multi-channel is where receiver-based systems are clearly stronger.
Tours, outdoor sites, moving groups Radio No fixed installation to design, and range follows the group.
A single service point — reception, counter, ticket window Counter loop Cheap, quick, no receivers, and disproportionately useful. Usually the first thing a small organisation should buy.
New build or major refurbishment Decide early, whatever the answer Loop cable is trivial to lay in an open floor and expensive to retrofit into a finished one. Raise it at design stage.

Auracast and what it changes

Auracast broadcast audio, built on Bluetooth LE Audio, lets a transmitter broadcast one or more audio streams that any number of nearby compatible receivers can join — hearing aids, earbuds, or phones. It addresses the historic weakness of receiver-based systems, which is that the venue has to supply, charge, clean, and chase receivers.

Three honest observations for a venue deciding today:

  • It depends on the listener's device. Benefit reaches only people whose hearing aids or earbuds support it, and the installed base of telecoil-equipped aids is very large and will remain in service for many years.
  • Joining a broadcast is a user-interface problem, not just a radio one — finding and selecting the right stream is a step that a loop simply does not have. Practice and signage conventions are still settling.
  • The accessible answer is usually "both". Where budget allows, an Auracast transmitter alongside an existing loop reaches new devices without abandoning the users who rely on telecoils. Removing a working loop to replace it with Auracast today would strand people.

Obligations

Requirements to provide assistive listening are set in law and differ by jurisdiction. Depending on where your venue is, rules may specify which spaces need a system, how many receivers must be available, how many must be hearing-aid compatible, what signage is required, and how coverage is defined. These are not optional design preferences and they change over time.

Find the specific provision that applies to your building and read it, or get advice from someone who has. Nothing on this site establishes what you are required to do — it exists to help you understand what you are being sold and to ask better questions about it.
