How you know it works

A hearing loop is invisible, inaudible to most of the people responsible for it, and used by people who are unlikely to complain to staff. That combination is why measurement is not optional and why "it was working when we installed it" is not a status report.

The standard

IEC 60118-4 is the international standard covering the performance of audio frequency induction loop systems for hearing aids. It is the document a commissioning report should reference, and it is what "compliant loop" ought to mean. Broadly, it specifies:

  • A reference field strength that the system should produce for an average programme level, expressed as a magnetic field strength in amperes per metre — commonly cited as 400 mA/m at 1 kHz, with the standard also addressing peak capability above that.
  • A frequency response tolerance — the field strength must stay within a specified band, commonly cited as ±3 dB, across a specified frequency range for speech intelligibility. This is the requirement that metal loss most often breaks.
  • A limit on background magnetic noise, so that hum and buzz from the building do not swamp the signal.
  • The measurement height and method, so that results are comparable — measurements are taken at a defined listening height rather than at the floor or at the loop.
Confirm the numbers against the standard itself The figures above are the ones generally quoted in the industry, but standards are revised, national annexes exist, and some jurisdictions impose their own requirements on top. Treat these values as orientation for understanding a report — not as a specification to procure against. Buy or consult the current published version, or require your installer to cite it explicitly and by edition.

What actually gets measured

Verification uses a calibrated field strength meter — an instrument with a reference pickup coil and defined weighting, not a hearing aid and not a phone app. Loop receivers sold as listening aids are useful for a quick presence check; they are not measuring instruments and will not tell you whether a room passes.

A commissioning engineer typically:

  1. Feeds a defined test signal through the system at a normal operating level.
  2. Measures field strength at a grid of points across the listening area, at the specified listening height, recording the value at each.
  3. Sweeps or steps through frequencies to confirm the response stays inside the tolerance band — particularly at the top of the range, where metal loss bites.
  4. Measures background magnetic noise with the system silent, and with the building in its normal operating state: lights on, dimmers at typical settings, HVAC running.
  5. Checks each source routed into the loop, not just the one that was convenient on the day.
  6. Records the results, the instrument used, its calibration date, and the standard and edition applied.

The walk test any venue can do

Measurement is a specialist job. Routine checking is not, and it is the part that decides whether the system is working on the day someone needs it. With an inexpensive loop listener — a small receiver with headphones, sold for exactly this purpose — a member of staff can, in ten minutes:

  1. Turn the system on and confirm any indicator light or level meter on the driver behaves as expected.
  2. Have someone speak into each microphone in turn, naming which one they are using.
  3. Walk the whole seating area with the listener, including the back row, the sides, and any balcony or overflow area, listening for level, clarity, and dead spots.
  4. Listen with nobody speaking, for hum or buzz — and note anything that has changed in the building since last time, such as new lighting.
  5. Check the areas outside the room where confidentiality matters, and note if the signal is audible there.
  6. Write the date, the result, and the tester's name in the venue's log.

Do this monthly if the room is in regular use, and always after electrical work, lighting changes, AV changes, or building work. Most loop faults are discovered this way, or not at all.

The failure modes a check catches

  • The driver is switched off, or its power has been unplugged for a vacuum cleaner and never restored. This is the single most common fault.
  • The source has been re-patched. Somebody rearranged the desk for an event and the loop feed never came back.
  • A microphone was replaced and its routing to the loop was not reinstated.
  • New lighting has introduced hum, often after an LED or dimmer retrofit.
  • The loop conductor has been damaged by building work — a drilled floor, a lifted carpet, a new partition.
  • A new partition wall has changed the room, and the field no longer covers the seats that exist now.
  • Nobody knows how to turn it on. Not a fault, but it fails identically from the user's side.

Make it someone's job

The durable arrangement in a small venue is: one named role owns the check, a one-page instruction lives with the equipment, the log is kept where the equipment is, and the periodic professional verification is booked on a recurring calendar entry rather than remembered. None of that is technical work, and it is what separates venues whose loops work from venues that have loops.
