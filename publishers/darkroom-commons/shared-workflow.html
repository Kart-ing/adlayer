Shared-space workflow

Most disputes in a shared darkroom are about equipment nobody has checked in a year. Two tests and one layout rule remove the majority of them.

Test the safelights

Safelight filters fade. Bulbs get replaced with brighter ones. A safelight moved closer to the bench during a rearrangement is now fogging every print in the room, and the symptom — muddy highlights and flat prints that everyone blames on the paper or the developer — points nowhere near the cause.

The coin test

  1. In full darkroom conditions with the safelights on as normal, lay a sheet of the paper you use on the baseboard, emulsion up, where you would normally work.
  2. Give it a brief exposure under the enlarger first — enough to bring it just to the threshold of a faint tone. Paper that has already been exposed is far more sensitive to fogging than raw paper, which is why testing raw paper understates the problem.
  3. Lay a few coins on the sheet and leave it under the safelights for a realistic working time — several minutes, matching how long paper is genuinely out during a session.
  4. Develop, fix and wash normally, then look at it in good light.
  5. If the coins have left visible discs — the covered areas lighter than their surroundings — your safelights are fogging paper. Move them further away, reduce the wattage, replace the filter, or reduce the number in use, and repeat.

Re-test whenever a bulb, filter, or fixture position changes, and once a year regardless. Note the date on the log. Also worth knowing: safelight colour must match the material. Filters suited to standard black-and-white printing paper will fog panchromatic film and some specialist papers, which must be handled in complete darkness.

Align the enlargers

An enlarger with negative stage, lens, and baseboard out of parallel cannot produce a print sharp across the whole frame at any aperture. Users respond by stopping down further and further and blaming the lens, which costs exposure time and does not fix it.

  • Check alignment with a proper alignment tool, or with a small mirror and a laser, or at minimum with a spirit level on each of the three planes.
  • Check it on a schedule, and after anything heavy has been moved on the bench.
  • Record the check in the equipment log with a date and initials, so nobody re-does it blindly and nobody assumes it was done.
  • While you are there: clean the condenser or diffuser, the negative carrier glass if any, and the lens. Dust in the optical path is a per-print tax on everyone who uses the machine.

Contamination discipline

Fixer carried backwards into developer is the classic shared-darkroom failure. It produces stains, blotches, and prematurely dead developer, and it is always attributed to bad chemistry rather than to hands.

  • One pair of tongs per tray, colour-coded or labelled, and they never move between trays.
  • Fixed tray order, left to right, marked on the bench so the layout does not depend on who set up.
  • Hands out of the trays. If hands go in, they get rinsed and dried before touching anything upstream — including the enlarger, the paper box, and the door handle.
  • Dry side and wet side stay separate. Enlargers, paper, negatives and the cutter on one side; trays, sink and wash on the other. Never put a wet print down on the dry bench.
  • Paper boxes stay closed and stay on the dry side. An open box in a room where someone is about to turn the light on wastes a lot of paper.

Washing and drying, which is where sessions collide

Washing is slow and everyone wants to leave at the same time. This is the real capacity constraint in most shared darkrooms and it is worth designing around.

  • Batch, do not trickle. Hold fixed prints in a holding tray of water, then wash them together at the end. This costs far less water and far less time than washing each print as it comes out.
  • Use a washing aid before the final wash, particularly for fibre-based paper, to cut wash time substantially.
  • Resin-coated and fibre-based paper wash very differently. Resin-coated paper clears quickly; fibre-based paper holds fixer in the paper base and needs much longer, and mixing them in one wash means either over-washing one or under-washing the other. Wash them separately.
  • Label the drying racks and screens with name and date, and set a rule for how long work may occupy them. Drying space that never clears is the most common source of friction in a community darkroom.
  • Do not dry fibre prints on a rack that has had fixer dripped on it. Screens get contaminated and then stain everything laid on them; wash the screens periodically.

Booking and handover

  • Book the room, not the enlarger, unless the room genuinely supports simultaneous independent users. Two people printing side by side works; two people where one needs total darkness does not.
  • Build set-up and clean-down into the slot. A two-hour booking with no allowance for either produces a room that is always mid-session.
  • Define what "left clean" means, in writing: trays emptied and rinsed, chemistry returned or discarded per the rule, bench wiped, screens clear, ventilation left running for the purge, log completed.
  • One log book, in the room. Chemistry mixed and discarded, fixer tests and clearing times, safelight and alignment checks, equipment faults, spills, waste containers filled and collected, and induction sign-offs. It is the room's memory and it is the only thing that makes the rest of this site enforceable.
A short list of house rules covers most of it Tongs never swap trays. Nothing goes down the drain that is not on the list. Chemistry is dated on mixing. The fixer is tested, not guessed. The light goes on before any spill is touched. The room is left as you would want to find it. Six lines on the wall settle more arguments than six pages in a handbook nobody opens.

Equipment worth having and often missing

  • A reliable thermometer, and a second one to check the first against.
  • Graduates in more than one size — accurate dilution at small volumes needs a small graduate.
  • A timer that is legible in safelight, for the wet bench, separate from the enlarger timer.
  • A grain focuser, so sharpness is confirmed rather than hoped for.
  • A print washer sized to the room's throughput, not to one enthusiast's habits.
  • A test-strip cutter or a straightedge, so nobody uses the guillotine in the dark.
  • A whiteboard by the door for the room's current state — what is mixed, what is broken, what is drying.
