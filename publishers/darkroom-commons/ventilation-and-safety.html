Ventilation and safety

A darkroom is a small, sealed, poorly lit room in which people handle chemicals in open trays for hours. Every part of that description is a reason to take ventilation seriously.

Why sealed rooms are the problem

Darkrooms are light-tight by design, which means they are usually also close to air-tight by accident. Vapour from open trays — acetic acid from the stop bath, sulphur compounds from the fixer — accumulates instead of dispersing. The common experience of a headache after an afternoon of printing is generally a ventilation symptom, not an unavoidable feature of the craft.

The other consequence is that a darkroom cannot be ventilated by simply opening a door or a window. Light-trapped intake and extract are what make a functioning darkroom possible at all, and retrofitting them is the single largest improvement most shared rooms can make.

How the air should move

  • Extract low, near the wet bench. Vapour from trays is heavier than room air and pools at the working surface. An extract fan high in the opposite corner pulls contaminated air through the operator's face on its way out.
  • Supply at the other end of the room, so air sweeps across the space rather than short-circuiting from intake straight to extract.
  • Both openings must be light-trapped — labyrinth baffles or purpose-made light-tight vents. A pair of light traps and a modest fan beats a powerful fan you can only run with the door open.
  • Keep the room slightly negative relative to the corridor, so vapour does not migrate into adjoining spaces.
  • Run the fan before and after, not only during. Give the room a purge cycle at the start and end of every session.
  • A slot-type local exhaust at the back of the wet bench is the substantial upgrade: it captures vapour at the tray rather than diluting it after it has already entered the room.

Ventilation rates for darkrooms are specified in institutional and industrial-hygiene guidance, usually as air changes per hour, and the applicable figure depends on your jurisdiction and on whether the space is in a school or a workplace. If your room is part of an institution, ask its safety officer for the number that applies rather than adopting one from a forum.

Mixing powders is the highest-risk step in the room Dry photographic chemicals become airborne dust when poured, and dust is inhaled far more readily than vapour. Where powdered products are used, mix them in a fume hood or outside the darkroom entirely, wear appropriate respiratory protection as directed by the product's safety data sheet, add powder to water rather than water to powder, and never mix during a printing session with other people in the room. Many shared darkrooms simply prohibit powders and buy liquid concentrates instead. That is a legitimate and common decision.

Protective equipment that people will actually use

ItemWhy
Tongs, one pair per tray The primary control. Keeping hands out of the trays prevents both skin contact and cross-contamination in a single habit.
Nitrile gloves For mixing, for cleaning up, and for anyone with sensitive skin. Note that some developing agents are recognised skin sensitisers: repeated exposure can produce a reaction in someone who has handled them for years without trouble.
Eye protection Mandatory for mixing and decanting concentrates. Concentrates are far more aggressive than working solutions.
Apron Cheap, and the reason people stop being casual about splashes.
Eyewash Accessible, in date, and known to every user before they need it.

The equipment only works if it is stocked. A shared room where the gloves box has been empty for a month has, in practice, no glove policy.

Storage and separation

  • Keep acids and alkalis apart, and keep concentrates in secondary containment — a tray or bund that would hold the contents if a bottle failed.
  • Store at low level. A five-litre bottle on a high shelf is a fall waiting to happen in a dark room.
  • Original containers, original labels. Where decanting is unavoidable, the new container gets a full label including the product name, dilution, and date.
  • Never in a drinks container. No exceptions, no "just for a minute".
  • Keep a copy of every safety data sheet in the room, and re-download them when you change products.

Spills

  1. Light comes on. Nobody cleans up a spill in safelight. A ruined print is cheaper than a fall.
  2. Ventilation to full, and open the light-trapped route if the spill is large.
  3. Contain with absorbent from the spill kit, working from the outside in.
  4. Collect into a labelled waste container, not the sink — see silver and waste.
  5. Wash the area, then restock the spill kit before the next session.
  6. Write it in the log, including what was spilled and how much. Repeated small spills at the same point usually mean a layout problem worth fixing.

The other hazards people forget

  • Water and electricity. Enlargers, timers, dryers and safelights all sit near a wet bench. Residual-current protection on darkroom circuits, and no trailing leads across the floor.
  • Working in the dark. Trip hazards, unexpected obstacles, and drawers left open are much more dangerous here than in a lit room. A rule that the floor stays clear is a safety rule, not a tidiness rule.
  • Lone working. A person alone in a dark, sealed room with chemicals is a genuine risk case. Many shared darkrooms require a second person on site, or a check-in procedure.
  • UV. If the room is also used for alternative processes with UV exposure units, that is a separate hazard with its own eye and skin protection requirements.

Induction for new users

A shared darkroom should not let anyone work unsupervised until they have been walked through: where the ventilation switch is, where the eyewash and spill kit are, which tongs belong to which tray, how the fixer test works, what goes into the waste container versus the drain, how to leave the room, and who to tell when something is wrong. Fifteen minutes, signed off in the log. It is the cheapest intervention available and it prevents most of what goes wrong.
