Chemistry management

In a private darkroom you know how old your fixer is. In a shared darkroom nobody does, unless the room is set up so that the answer is written on the bottle.

What each bath is for

Developer
Reduces exposed silver halide to metallic silver, producing the image. It is the step where time, temperature, agitation, and dilution all interact, and the step where inconsistency shows up most visibly. Developers are the least stable of the three: they oxidise in contact with air, and diluted working solutions have short lives.
Stop bath
An acidic bath that halts development immediately and prevents developer being carried into the fixer, where it shortens fixer life. Usually dilute acetic acid, which smells strongly, or citric-acid-based products, which do not. A plain water rinse is sometimes used instead, particularly for film, but it stops development more slowly and carries more developer forward.
Fixer
Dissolves the remaining undeveloped silver halide so the image is no longer light-sensitive. Under-fixing is the single most common cause of prints and negatives that discolour months later, and it is invisible on the day. Fixer is the bath most worth testing, because it exhausts gradually rather than failing obviously.
Washing aid / hypo clearing agent
An optional bath after fixing that helps residual fixer clear from the emulsion and paper base, cutting wash times substantially — which matters both for archival permanence and for water use. Especially worthwhile with fibre-based paper, which holds fixer in the paper base far more stubbornly than resin-coated paper.

One-shot or replenished

One-shotReplenished / re-used
How it works Mix working solution from concentrate, use once, discard. Keep a working tank; top up with replenisher or extend time as capacity is consumed.
Consistency Very high. Every user gets identical chemistry. Depends entirely on accurate record-keeping.
Cost and waste Higher concentrate use, more volume discarded. Lower cost per roll, less liquid waste.
Fit for a shared room Strongly preferred for film developer. It removes the argument entirely. Workable for print developer and fixer in trays during a session, with a log.

The practical compromise most shared darkrooms land on: film developer strictly one-shot; print developer mixed fresh for a session and discarded at the end of it; stop and fixer used across a session with a tested capacity limit.

Capacity and exhaustion

Every chemical has a rated capacity from its manufacturer — a number of rolls or a number of sheets of a given size per litre. Those figures are the starting point, not the answer, because capacity also degrades with time and air exposure whether or not you use it.

Testing fixer: the clip test

The most useful minute in a shared darkroom.

  1. Take an unexposed scrap of the film you are using — the leader trimmed off a roll is ideal — and drop it into the fixer in room light.
  2. Time how long it takes to go from milky to fully clear. That is the clearing time.
  3. With fresh fixer, note the clearing time and write it on the bottle. That is your baseline.
  4. Retest periodically. When the clearing time reaches roughly twice the fresh baseline, the fixer is done — replace it.
  5. Conventional practice is to fix film for at least twice the clearing time.

The same principle underlies commercial fixer test solutions, which indicate dissolved silver directly and are convenient for paper fixer where the clip test does not apply as neatly.

Under-fixed is not visible on the day A print that was not fixed long enough, or was fixed in exhausted fixer, looks completely normal when it dries. It yellows and stains over months. This is why a shared darkroom needs a fixer rule that does not depend on any individual's judgement — a tested baseline, a written capacity, and a replacement date.

Temperature

Black-and-white processing is conventionally standardised at 20 °C (68 °F), and published development times assume it. Development rate rises with temperature, so a few degrees changes contrast and density noticeably.

  • Measure it every time. A thermometer in the working solution, not an assumption about the room.
  • Match the whole sequence. Large temperature differences between developer, stop, fix and wash can stress the emulsion. Keep them reasonably close.
  • Compensate rather than guess. Manufacturers publish time–temperature charts. If the room runs warm in summer, shorten the time per the chart rather than accepting whatever comes out.
  • A tempering bath is the low-tech fix. A tray or sink of water at 20 °C holding the bottles, prepared before the session starts.

Agitation

Agitation replaces exhausted developer at the emulsion surface. Too little produces uneven development and streaking from the sprocket holes; too much raises contrast and can cause surge marks. What matters most in a shared room is that everyone uses the same scheme, written on the wall — for example, continuous for the first thirty seconds, then a set number of inversions at the start of each minute. Any consistent scheme beats an inconsistent good one, because the development times you calibrate are only valid for the agitation you calibrated them with.

Storage

  • Fill bottles to the top. Oxidation is driven by the air in the bottle. Collapsible bottles, floating lids, or displacing the headspace with an inert gas all extend concentrate life; so does decanting into several small full bottles instead of one half-empty large one.
  • Keep it dark and cool, away from heat sources and direct sun.
  • Label everything, in permanent marker, on the container itself. Never store photographic chemistry in a drinks bottle — this is the most consistently repeated rule in every darkroom safety policy, and it is repeated because the mistake keeps happening.
  • Keep the safety data sheets in the room, in a folder anyone can reach, not in a filing cabinet in an office.
