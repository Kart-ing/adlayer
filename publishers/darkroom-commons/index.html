Running a darkroom that ten people share

There is a great deal written about how to develop a roll of film. There is very little written about how to run a darkroom that twenty different people use in a week, each of whom believes the last person left the fixer exhausted.

A shared darkroom — in a school, a co-op, an arts centre, a community studio — fails in ways a private darkroom does not. Chemistry that nobody owns goes unlogged and then goes bad. Trays get cross-contaminated. Safelights drift out of specification and nobody notices until a whole term's prints look flat. Waste accumulates in a corner because nobody is sure whether it can go down the drain. None of these are photographic problems. They are operating problems, and they have operating answers.

These notes cover black-and-white film and paper processing, which is what most shared darkrooms actually run. Colour processing has tighter temperature control and different waste handling, and is mostly out of scope here.

The habits that separate a working darkroom from a frustrating one

  • Date every bottle. Mixing date, dilution, and a running count of what has been processed through it. Chemistry with no date is chemistry nobody will trust or use.
  • Test the fixer, do not guess it. A clip test takes under a minute and settles the single most common argument in a shared darkroom.
  • One set of tongs per tray, and they never swap. Fixer carried backwards into developer is the leading cause of mysterious stains and dead developer.
  • Re-test safelights on a schedule. Filters fade and bulbs get replaced with the wrong wattage. The test is simple and it is the only way to know.
  • Write things in the log. Not because anyone enjoys it, but because in a shared space the log is the only memory the room has.
A working temperature to standardise on Black-and-white processing is conventionally standardised at 20 °C (68 °F), and most published development times assume it. Holding that temperature — with a tempering bath, or simply by letting chemistry sit in the room until it equalises — removes an entire class of inconsistency before anyone touches a timer. If you cannot hold it, at minimum measure it, and use the manufacturer's time–temperature compensation rather than hoping.

Scope, and where these notes stop

Photographic chemicals are real chemicals. Some are irritants, some are sensitisers, and a few are seriously hazardous in concentrated or powdered form. Every product you use has a safety data sheet from its manufacturer, and that document — not this site, and not a forum post — is the authority on how to handle, store, and dispose of it.

Waste discharge is regulated, and the rules differ by country, state, and even by individual sewer authority. This site explains why spent fixer is treated the way it is and what questions to ask; it deliberately does not tell you what you are permitted to pour down a drain, because that answer is local and only your regulator can give it.
