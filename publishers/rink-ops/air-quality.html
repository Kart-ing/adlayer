Air and the building envelope

An ice rink is a large cold surface inside a building full of warm, moist air. That single fact produces fog, ceiling condensation, frost on the ice, and a chunk of the energy bill. It is also the system most often ignored at small rinks, because it does not have a gauge anyone watches.

Why humidity is the problem

Warm air holds more moisture than cold air. When humid air in the bowl reaches the cold ice surface or a cold structural surface, the moisture has to go somewhere. It shows up as:

  • Fog over the ice — moisture condensing in the cold layer of air just above the sheet. At its worst it obscures the far end of the rink and becomes a safety issue, not just a nuisance.
  • Frost or a white bloom on the ice surface — moisture depositing directly onto the sheet, which the resurfacer then has to remove.
  • Condensation and drip from the ceiling and structure — the ceiling is cold because it radiates heat to the ice below. Once it drops below the dew point of the air near it, it wets, and water drips onto the sheet, freezing into bumps. In sustained cases it causes corrosion of structural steel and damage to insulation, which is a far more expensive problem than bumpy ice.
  • Slippery floors and rusting fittings in the spaces around the bowl.

Moisture arrives from outdoor air infiltration, from open doors, from spectators, from flood water, and from combustion if you run fuel-fired equipment indoors. Humid summer weather is the hardest case for a rink that runs year-round, and it is exactly when small rinks assume the ice problem must be refrigeration.

Dehumidification

Rinks generally use one of two approaches, sometimes both:

ApproachHow it worksWhere it fits
Mechanical / refrigerant Cools air below its dew point on a coil, condenses moisture out, reheats. Effective at warmer, more humid conditions. Loses effectiveness as the air gets cold, because there is less moisture to condense and the coil can frost.
Desiccant Passes air over a desiccant wheel or bed that adsorbs moisture, then regenerates the desiccant with heat and exhausts the moisture outside. Works at the low temperatures found in a rink bowl, which is why desiccant units are common in arenas.

Whichever you have, two operator points apply. First, a dehumidifier that is running is not necessarily working — check that it is actually removing moisture, by watching the humidity in the bowl, not the run light. Second, its filters, its regeneration air path, and its exhaust matter; a blocked exhaust on a desiccant unit dumps the moisture straight back into the building.

Low-emissivity ceilings

The ice surface radiates heat to the ceiling and the ceiling radiates back. A low-emissivity ceiling — a reflective surface installed under the roof structure — reduces that radiant exchange. Two things follow: the refrigeration load drops, and the ceiling itself stays warmer, which makes condensation and drip less likely. It is a well-established retrofit in arenas and one of the few capital items that pays back in both energy and ice quality at once.

If your rink has one, do not damage it hanging banners and lighting from it, and do not let it fill with dust — a dirty reflective surface is a less reflective surface.

Combustion gases: the part that is a health issue

Fuel-fired ice resurfacers and edgers — propane, natural gas, petrol — operate inside a large, deliberately sealed, cold building. Their exhaust contains carbon monoxide (CO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and both accumulate in the bowl if ventilation is inadequate. This is not a theoretical risk. Poisoning incidents at ice arenas are documented in the public-health literature, they have affected players, spectators and staff, and NO2 in particular is dangerous because its effects can be delayed by hours, so a rink can send everyone home before anyone realises what happened.

The controls are well understood:

  • Maintain and tune the engine. An out-of-tune or poorly maintained engine can produce dramatically more CO than the same engine running correctly. Emissions testing on a schedule, with results recorded, is the core control.
  • Ventilate on a rule, not on a feeling. Run mechanical ventilation during and after resurfacing, and set the schedule so it accounts for back-to-back floods during tournaments — the highest-risk scenario is the busiest day.
  • Monitor. Fixed CO and NO2 monitors in the bowl, with alarms, calibrated and tested on the manufacturer's interval. A monitor that has never been calibrated is decoration.
  • Do not idle indoors. Warm-up, refuelling, and idling belong outside or in a properly ventilated bay.
  • Consider electric. Battery-electric resurfacers remove the source entirely, which is why many arenas have moved to them. They change the operating routine — charging time, weight, run time per charge — so plan the schedule around the machine rather than assuming a direct swap.
Check your jurisdiction Many jurisdictions set specific requirements for ice arenas: permissible CO and NO2 concentrations, monitoring, ventilation rates, resurfacer emissions testing intervals, and record-keeping. These vary and they change. Find the rule that applies to your building, in writing, and keep the records it asks for. Nothing on this page substitutes for it.

Doors, envelope, and the boring wins

  • Close the doors. A propped loading door on a humid day undoes a dehumidifier. This is the most common single cause of a sudden fog or frost problem at a small rink.
  • Seal the envelope. Gaps around doors, penetrations, and old vents let humid air in continuously and are cheap to fix.
  • Watch the lighting. Older high-intensity fixtures put significant radiant heat into the sheet. LED retrofits reduce both the electrical load and the ice load, and they restrike instantly, which lets you actually turn them off between sessions.
  • Turn things off between sessions. Lights, ventilation above what is needed, and spectator-area heating all add to the load on an empty building.

A short diagnostic order

  1. Fog, frost, or ceiling drip appears → measure relative humidity at rink level before touching the refrigeration setpoint.
  2. Humidity high → is the dehumidifier running, and is it removing water? Check filters, exhaust path, and regeneration.
  3. Dehumidifier fine → check doors, recent weather, event attendance, and anything new in the building.
  4. Ceiling dripping specifically → the ceiling surface is below dew point. Reduce humidity first; consider a low-emissivity ceiling as the durable fix.
  5. Headaches, nausea, or coughing reported after skating → treat as a possible combustion-gas exposure immediately. Ventilate, get people out, check monitors and the resurfacer, and involve the people your jurisdiction requires you to involve. Do not wait for a pattern.
