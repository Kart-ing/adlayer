Building ice, and keeping it worth skating on

Ice quality is governed by two numbers an operator controls directly — thickness and surface temperature — and by the care taken in the first few hours of the build, which nothing later can fully undo.

The build, in order

Ice is built in thin layers because thin layers freeze cleanly and bond. Flooding heavily to save time produces cloudy, weak, poorly bonded ice with trapped air, and every defect frozen in on day one stays there until the sheet is stripped in spring.

  1. Bring the slab down first. The concrete has to be cold and stable before water touches it. Flooding a warm slab wastes water and time and produces poor bonding.
  2. Seal coats. The first passes are very light — often applied as a spray rather than a flood — so they freeze almost on contact and seal the slab surface. Water applied faster than it can freeze will pool and run.
  3. Whitening. Ice paint or a white sealer layer is built up next. Its purpose is visibility and contrast, not decoration: a puck and a skate edge are far easier to see against white than against grey concrete. Apply and freeze in several thin coats rather than one thick one.
  4. Lines, logos, markings. Applied on top of the white base while it is still thin, then locked in by more clear layers. Every subsequent layer is a decision you cannot revisit without stripping.
  5. Clear build-up. Repeated thin floods until the sheet reaches working thickness. This is the long, dull part of the build and the part most often rushed.
Water quality changes ice quality Dissolved minerals and dissolved gases both affect how ice freezes and how it behaves under a skate. Rinks with hard water often treat or filter flood water, and many use hot water for resurfacing partly because it carries less dissolved gas. Whether treatment is worth the cost is a local question — but if your ice behaves differently from a neighbouring rink using the same equipment and the same schedule, water is a reasonable place to look.

Thickness: measure it, do not estimate it

Working ice on an indoor sheet is commonly kept in the region of one to one and a half inches — roughly 25 to 38 mm. Precision matters less than consistency and knowing the actual figure.

Too thick and the ice insulates itself from the cold slab: the plant runs longer, the surface stays warm and soft anyway, and energy cost climbs. Too thin and the resurfacer blade reaches paint, the sheet tears at faceoff dots and goal creases, and a repair becomes a rebuild.

How to measure

  • Use a depth gauge — a drill bit with a stop and a hooked probe — at a fixed grid of marked points, so numbers are comparable week to week.
  • Include the areas that wear fastest: goal creases, faceoff circles, the gate area, and wherever the resurfacer turns.
  • Plug the holes. Record the numbers. Compare to last week, not to a target.

Thickness maps drift predictably: high where the resurfacer dwells, low where skates work. When the map is more than a modest range from thin to thick, the fix is usually resurfacer technique — pattern, water volume, blade setting — not more flooding.

Surface temperature: the lever you actually have

Colder ice is harder and faster and more brittle. Warmer ice is softer and slower and takes an edge more forgivingly. That trade-off is why hockey and figure skating want different sheets:

UseTypical directionWhy
Hockey Colder, harder ice Speed, puck slide, and a surface that holds up under heavy traffic and repeated stops.
Figure skating Warmer, softer ice Jump landings bite into the surface rather than shattering it; toe picks and deep edges need ice that yields slightly.
Public skate / learn-to-skate Between the two Forgiving to beginners, tolerant of heavy snow production and frequent stops.
Curling Its own discipline entirely Pebbled surface, very different preparation and tolerances; a shared hockey sheet is always a compromise for it.

A single-sheet community rink that hosts hockey in the evening and a skating club in the morning cannot fully satisfy both. The honest approach is to pick a setpoint that serves the largest user group, tell the other groups plainly what the sheet is set for, and move it only when the schedule justifies the hours the plant needs to respond.

Measuring surface temperature

  • An infrared thermometer is the practical tool. Take readings at the same marked points each time, from the same height and angle.
  • Infrared readings on ice are sensitive to angle and to reflections from lights and warm ceilings. Consistency of method matters more than absolute accuracy — you are watching for change.
  • Measure after the ice has recovered from a resurface, not immediately behind the machine, or you are measuring the flood water.
  • Changing setpoint is slow. The slab, the coolant loop, and the ice all have thermal mass. Expect hours, not minutes, and do not chase it with repeated adjustments.

Reading common complaints

SymptomLook at first
Soft, slow, heavy snow production Ice too warm, or ice too thick and insulating the surface. Check surface temperature and the thickness map before touching the plant.
Brittle, chipping, spraying badly under a stop Ice too cold, or very dry air pulling the surface hard. Consider raising the setpoint slightly.
Frost or a fine white bloom forming on the surface Humidity in the bowl. This is a dehumidification question, not a refrigeration one — see air and envelope.
Ridges, waves, or a washboard finish Resurfacer blade, blade setting, or driving pattern. See the resurfacer routine.
Wet or slushy patches that will not freeze off Too much water laid down, poor drainage of the flood, or a local warm spot — check for a failed slab sensor, a lighting hot spot, or a door.
Paint showing through in the creases Thin ice. Stop shaving those areas, add thin floods, and re-measure before the next event.

End of season

Melting a sheet out is not just a matter of shutting the plant off. Ice is usually broken up and removed mechanically before or alongside the melt, because a full-sheet melt dumps a very large volume of water into the building drainage system at once. Plan where that water goes, protect the slab and dasher bases from the equipment used to break it, and use the bare slab window to inspect the floor, the header trench, and everything you cannot see for the rest of the year.
