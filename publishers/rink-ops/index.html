Operating notes for a single sheet of ice

Most of what is written about ice rinks is written for arenas with a full-time chief engineer. This site is for the other kind: one sheet, a seasonal budget, a part-time operator, and a board that wants to know why the ice was soft on Saturday.

A community rink is a refrigeration plant, a building envelope, and a sheet of ice that is thinner than most people assume, all interacting continuously. Almost every complaint an operator hears — slow ice, chippy ice, fog over the surface, water dripping from the ceiling, a resurfacer that leaves ridges — traces back to one of four systems. This site covers those four, in the order they cause problems.

The four numbers to know before anything else

ReadingWhy it mattersWhere it comes from
Ice thickness Thick ice insulates the surface from the slab, so the plant runs longer and the surface still skates soft. Thin ice tears and exposes paint. Drill gauge, on a fixed grid, weekly
Ice surface temperature The single lever you have over hardness. Hockey wants colder ice than figure skating; a shared sheet is always a compromise. Infrared thermometer, several points, same points each time
Secondary coolant supply temperature What the plant is actually delivering under the slab. Drifting supply temperature explains most slow-onset ice problems. Plant room gauge or controller log
Relative humidity in the bowl Drives fog, ceiling drip, and frost on the surface. A dehumidification problem looks exactly like a refrigeration problem to a skater. Hygrometer at rink level, away from doors
Keep a log, even a bad one A clipboard with date, outside conditions, coolant supply temperature, ice surface temperature, and anything unusual is worth more than any single instrument. Nearly every diagnosis on this site depends on comparing today to a normal day, and you cannot do that from memory.

What this site does not cover

Refrigeration plants using ammonia are regulated equipment in most jurisdictions, with mandatory training, alarm, ventilation, and record-keeping requirements. Nothing here substitutes for your operating permit, your manufacturer's manuals, or your refrigeration contractor. Where a topic crosses into work that a licensed technician should do, this site says so and stops.

The same goes for structural questions about the building, electrical work, and anything involving confined-space entry. This site is written to make an operator a better observer and a better communicator with the professionals who do that work — not to replace them.
